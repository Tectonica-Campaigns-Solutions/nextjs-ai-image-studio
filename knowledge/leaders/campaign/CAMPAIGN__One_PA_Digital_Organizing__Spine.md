# One PA Digital Activist Development Pilot, Campaign Spine

**What this document is.** The sequence of the program, for orienting a digital organizer: which phase they are in, what it is for, what to actually do, where leaders get stuck, and how to know it is working. Read it on every orient turn and on the first turn of every conversation.

**How the phases work.** There are four phases plus a leadership track that runs under all of them. The phases overlap. They are shifts in where the weight of the work sits, not stages that finish. A group can be building itself and mobilizing its base in the same week. Do not tell a leader they have "completed" a phase or that they are "behind" because a date has passed; locate them by what their group is actually doing.

**The underlying model.** Three motions run at once. Engagement: people move from interested, to participating, to leading. Conversations: people move in alignment, from opposed, through persuadable, toward the base. Leadership growth: capacity builds in the people doing the work. Relationships connect the first two, and belonging drives all of it. People come back because of each other; belonging comes before belief.

**The operating frame: mobilize the base to persuade the middle.** Groups first learn to act together in spaces that already agree with them, then carry that competence into spaces that do not. Every piece of work starts by naming which kind of space it is in, base or persuadable middle, and choosing the intention accordingly.

## Leadership track (runs under every phase)

**What it is for.** A leader is not someone who takes a lot of action. A leader is someone who moves other people to act and holds them together through shared purpose. Activists become leaders by leading, and they do it as a cohort, not alone.

**What the leader does.** Takes on the role and the identity, not a task list: pulling people together because they know them, making the ask, following up on the ordinary problems of the people they lead. Brings problems to other leaders in the leader feed, gives and takes honest feedback on plans, and trades what is working. Keeps in regular contact with One PA.

**Where leaders get stuck.** Treating the role as doing more themselves rather than moving others. Going it alone instead of using the other leaders. Waiting for instructions on the how, when the how is usually theirs.

**How you know it is working.** The leader is recruiting, planning, making calls in fast-moving moments, and holding the group through a bad week. They talk with other leaders, not only with One PA.

**Healthy rhythm, as One PA defines it.** In crunch periods: the leader talks with members daily, with One PA every two or three days, and the group does something together every week. In build and maintenance periods, those intervals stretch by two or three times. Missing any of these is an early warning, and communication slipping shows up before output does.

## Phase: Engage, build your group (Sept 29 to Oct 10)

**What it is for.** Leaders identify their group members, make the ask, bring them together, build relationships and run the first meeting. The point is that these people get to know each other and act as people who belong together, not as a set of task-doers. Meetings can be online or in person.

**What the leader does.**
- Builds the group from their own network, five to fifteen people, through personal asks. One ask is rarely enough; plan for several.
- Runs the first meeting as a real meeting, not a task list: people get to know each other, and the group starts shaping what it is for.
- Learns One PA's guardrails and practices, and chooses the group's own activities.
- Maps where the conversations on the group's issues actually happen: local news and its comments, social spaces, and the private Signal, WhatsApp and discussion groups members know about and the organization does not. This map is an asset by week four.
- Toward the end of the phase, may put up small local petitions or simple statements of agreement on local issues, many rather than one, as a door into the organization. These go through approval where One PA requires it.
- Practices a first response on something easy before anything is live.

**Where leaders get stuck.** Stopping after one ask. Running the first meeting as a briefing. Treating the group as a distribution list. A slow first week: the program is five weeks long and a slow start cannot be made up later, so group activity in week one is the signal to watch.

**How you know it is working.** The group has met, people know each other by name, there is a first sense of what the group is for, and there is a starting map of the group's spaces.

**Sibling routing.** The Writing eCoach for drafting the ask. The Events eCoach, once available, for setting up a get-together.

## Phase: Mobilize the base (Oct 6 to 19)

**What it is for.** The group builds competence together in spaces that already agree with it, before harder terrain. This is the organizing: the group plans, coordinates and acts as one.

**What the leader does.**
- Makes a general plan for the phase and a way of responding to things as they come, then turns the plan into work assigned to members by role, with owners and dates.
- Coordinates messaging inside the group: the boost and share, and the next level, "I'm in an argument here, can someone come in and back me up."
- Uses the group's internal space to share ideas, review each other's drafts and pitch things to each other before going public.
- Decides with the group how to handle trolls: whether to engage at all is a judgment call, and responding strategically beats responding defensively.
- Aligns friendly spaces on a shared narrative in the group's own words, and makes room for people outside the group who want to help.
- Supports bridging across differences: people who share a common threat but not a common language or identity, relating human experience to human experience.

**Where leaders get stuck.** Plans that stay documents instead of becoming assigned work. All coordination running through the leader, so the group turns into a broadcast list. Posting a lot into echo chambers and mistaking volume for impact: eight real exchanges do more than forty posts, even though the numbers will say the opposite.

**How you know it is working.** Members are acting on the plan without the leader pushing each step, backing each other up in real time, and bringing ideas to the group.

**Pacing.** This phase ends at the October 19 registration deadline. Conversations with people in the group's networks who may not be registered belong before that date.

## Phase: Persuade the middle (Oct 13 to 27)

**What it is for.** This is where the leadership and relationships built so far show. Groups talk with people who think differently and are persuadable, without alienating them: calling people in rather than pushing them out. Persuasion by organizations goes badly; persuasion by trusted people in their own communities is what works.

**What the leader does.**
- Brings the Four Keys into conversations with people who disagree: get personal, go local, be human, build leadership.
- Localizes: takes a general issue and works out how it lands here, at the local hospital, in the problems people already see.
- Maps the invisible networks with the group: places that are not political and out of sight of the organization, where people who consider themselves apolitical gather. Decides with the group where a message needs to be heard even if unwelcome, and where it should stay out.
- Speaks in values and impacts, not policy handed down. Asks about the other person's experience and leaves room for them to arrive somewhere themselves.
- Handles rapid response within the day when a candidate or issue moment breaks.

**Where leaders get stuck.** Defensiveness. Arguing policy instead of impact. Attacking someone who is not a fair target under One PA's guardrails. Using the organization's talking points word for word instead of in their own voice.

**How you know it is working.** Real conversations are happening with people who were not already aligned, in spaces that are not political.

**Pacing.** This phase ends at the mail ballot application deadline, 5pm October 27.

## Phase: Get out the vote (Oct 27 to Nov 3)

**What it is for.** Each group designs its own local turnout plan, reaching the people in its own networks who are on the fence about participating at all. One PA calls them high-potential.

**What the leader does.**
- Works with the group to identify who in their networks is wavering, and matches the right messenger to each person.
- Helps people make a vote plan: how they will get there, whether they have a vote buddy, and who the champions are who can bring several people with them. For any detail about how, when or where to vote, the group points people to official sources.
- Designs localized turnout activity through social spaces, by people who know the community.
- Reminds people not only that the election is coming but why it matters this time.
- Treats it as an experiment and records what happened.

**Where leaders get stuck.** Broadcasting reminders instead of personal contact. Taking on voting-mechanics questions themselves instead of pointing to official sources.

**How you know it is working.** Group members are having personal turnout conversations with specific people they know, and tracking who has a plan.

**Sibling routing.** A dedicated GOTV eCoach is not available. Help the group plan from the why and the relationships; route message drafting to the Writing eCoach.

## Fundraising (optional, from mid-October)

Offered, not assigned. A group may choose to fundraise close to the election, for example by turning one of its own get-togethers into a fundraiser. Funds are received through One PA, which requires a short coordination step before any group starts. If a leader raises it, point them to Jeffrey Lichtenstein first.

## Cross-cutting: the response cycle

Once the program is running, most work moves through the same cycle:

1. Something happens around one of One PA's issues: a candidate's record surfaces, a local story breaks, an aligned voice is attacked, a falsehood starts to travel.
2. One PA issues an assignment to one or several groups. It states the objective and the level of autonomy the group has. It often does not say how; the how is usually the group's.
3. The group develops its response plan and talking points in its own space: what to say, where, from whom. It can share the plan with other leaders for feedback.
4. Where One PA requires sign-off for that kind of work, the plan goes to Jeffrey Lichtenstein or Diamante Ortiz for a quick ready-or-needs-work check. Most things do not need it.
5. The plan becomes to-dos for the team, with the brief.
6. Where candidates are involved, groups act on One PA's line and briefings. Coordination with any campaign is One PA's to manage, not the group's.
7. Members find where it is being discussed; the group decides which spaces to enter.
8. The group makes what it needs, fitted to each platform, in its own words.
9. The group engages, coordinated, in the right register for each space, backing each other up.
10. Reflect and perfect: how did it go, what would make it better next time. Report back to One PA and to each other; wins go in the leader feed.

## Cross-cutting rules

- Belonging first. Coach toward groups that could outlast November 3.
- The how is the group's. One PA sets goals, guardrails and approval areas; inside those, leaders and groups decide.
- Field is out of scope. Canvassing, door knocking, phone banking and real-world voter recruitment route to Jeffrey. Real-world opportunities can be amplified by pointing people to where One PA manages field.
- Guardrails are One PA's. When explaining a rule, attribute it to One PA, never to an individual.
